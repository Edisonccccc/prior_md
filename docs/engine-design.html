§ 0Background: what this project is solving

If this is your first read, this section is the three minutes that make the rest of the document make sense. Unfamiliar with the vocabulary? The glossary is §12, at the end. Only here for the design? Keep going.

A real situation

A patient goes home from hospital, but the infection is not finished and they need intravenous antibiotics every day. The drug is expensive. Before the insurer pays, it wants one thing: proof that this is medically necessary, stated in our format.

That proof is called prior authorization (PA). The person who produces it is a licensed PA Specialist — the SME (Subject Matter Expert) of this project. Every abbreviation in this document is in the quick-reference at the top of §12. She receives a stack of referral material — the physician's order, clinical notes, culture and sensitivity results — and has to produce three things from it:

  • A PA one-liner — drug, strength, frequency, total vials, billing codes and units, start and end dates, all in the wording the payer accepts
  • Answers to the payer's questionnaire (CMM, CoverMyMeds, the portal most submissions go through) — mostly Yes/No, but nearly half the questions are deliberately left blank, because the questionnaire is a branching tree. This is more involved than it sounds; §6 is about it.
  • The diagnosis code — the one that decides approval

Then she submits and waits. Approved, the patient gets the drug. Denied, the patient waits, or pays, or appeals and waits several more weeks.

What the work looks like

Below is a real one-liner in structure (the numbers are from the project's case-001, de-identified). Take it apart and the whole architecture explains itself:

Why it is worth automating, and why it is dangerous

Look at the figure again: three of the four outputs are pure arithmetic — counting a calendar, multiplying doses, rounding up, converting billing units. That part is mechanical, repetitive, easy to get wrong when tired, and done many times a day. That is the case for automating it.

Where the danger is

The remaining one — the diagnosis code — decides approval. And an LLM (Large Language Model) happens to be extremely good at producing an ICD code that sounds entirely reasonable, and equally good at filling in a blank when the information is thin.

This is not hypothetical. In experiment #1 on 2026-07-30, a model given every rule got the arithmetic completely right and reproduced the hardest judgment correctly. But it wrote in T81.42XA (deep incisional) — when the chart never stated a depth at all. It also answered "Yes" to the generic-substitution question, reasoning it out by inference. About 85% overall — and the wrong 15% sat squarely on the most expensive part.

This whole design document is, at bottom, an answer to one question: how do you get something that fills in blanks to work somewhere blanks must not be filled in.

Who decides what

  • SME — the domain authority. Her rulings override the source document; where they conflict, the later word is hers. She does not write code, but everything she says has to become a test.
  • Edison — builds the system. Engineering judgment is his. Domain judgment is not.
  • The reviewing specialist (possibly the SME herself, possibly someone else) — reviews every output before it is submitted. This is not a temporary safety measure, it is the shape of the product.
The goal is not to make every case look approvable. The goal is to find exactly where a payer is likely to challenge it — while there is still time to fix it. SME · Q6 · this line works as a one-sentence definition of the product

§ 1Six things that cannot move

These are not preferences. They are the reasons the system has the shape it has. (SME = Subject Matter Expert — in this project, the PA specialist. She appears constantly from here on.) Any refactor has to leave all six intact.

  1. Deterministic work in code, judgment work in constrained LLM calls. From experiment #1: the model's arithmetic was perfect, but it reasoned its way into specificity the chart never stated. So the maths sank and the judgment was pulled up and constrained.
  2. Zero trust: the LLM's output is testimony, not fact. Fabrication halts the pipeline; an honest "I don't know" passes with a warning. The difference between those two is the pivot the whole design turns on.
  3. A fixed pipeline, not a free agent. In a medical workflow, auditability is a feature, not overhead.
  4. Never guess. An unknown drug, an unknown frequency, a code that never appears in the documents → flag it, do not generate it.
  5. Every SME-approved case becomes a permanent eval. Every change has to keep them all green.
  6. The specialist reviews everything. Full autonomy is an explicit non-goal.
Where this document stands

For every decision below I tried to make the property enforced by a type or a structure rather than requested in a sentence of prompt. Anything held together by "the model should remember not to…" will fail on some case.

§ 2System overview

Seven stations, one direction. The only fork is at station ②, and that fork is the pivot of the whole design.

§ 3The core type: values that carry their evidence

The single most important type in the system is this one:

# an extracted value is never a bare scalar
type Sourced[T] = Known[T] | Missing

@dataclass
class Known[T]:
value: T
quote: str # verbatim; must be findable in the source document
doc: DocId # which document
span: tuple[int,int] | None

@dataclass
class Missing:
reason: "not_documented" | "unclear" | "conflicting"
note: str # what the model saw that made it say so
Decision D-1 · evidence travels with the value

Rather than a "citations table" sitting alongside the JSON, the quote goes inside the value itself. The reason: any downstream code that wants to read the value must first confront where it came from. The rule "do not infer" stops being a line of prompt and becomes a type constraint — Missing has no .value to read, so forgetting to handle it is a crash, not a silent zero.

The cost: roughly 3x the JSON, and a longer extraction prompt.Worth it, because in experiment #1 the model answered "Yes" precisely by inference — and that failure is completely invisible in flat JSON.

What a whole Case looks like

Case
├─ documents: Document[] # the originals: read-only, never modified
├─ extraction: Extraction # ① everything is Sourced[...]
├─ validation: Verdict # ② halt | pass_with_warnings
├─ calculation: Calculation # ③ pure function, replayable
├─ judgment: Judgment | None # ④ None = not built / skipped
├─ findings: Finding[] # ⑤ preflight results
└─ worksheet: Worksheet # what the specialist actually reads

Every layer appends and never revises: ③ does not go back and change ②'s verdict, ④ does not recompute ③'s numbers. That is what makes any worksheet fully replayable from documents, and what lets a failure be pinned to one step of one layer.

§ 4Calculation: the schedule as an intermediate representation

The code today reads total_doses = per_day × days. The SME rejected that outright in Q8:

never estimate unusual frequencies using a generic daily multiplier. Build the administration schedule from the SOC date forward, count each actual dose that falls within the treatment period. SME · Q8 · 2026-08-11

The fix is not a few more entries in the frequency table. It is a different intermediate representation.

@dataclass
class Phase:
dose_mg: float
frequency: Frequency # q24h q12h q8h q6h q48h weekly once
duration: Days(n) | Doses(n)

@dataclass
class Regimen:
start: date
phases: list[Phase] # loading + maintenance = 2 phases; a taper = 3

def schedule(r: Regimen) -> list[Administration]:
"""Built forward from the SOC date, one entry per dose. The only place in the system that produces a dose count."""

Every number after this is a fold over that schedule — except day_supply, which is not:

A spec error found while implementing · 2026-08-26

This document originally defined day_supply as "last dose date − start date + 1". That is wrong. Her Q8 answer says the day supply remains 10 days because that is the length of the treatment period — a q48h course over 10 days has its last dose on day 9, and the day supply is still 10, not 9.

So day supply comes from the treatment length the order states (the sum of the phases' days); only dose count comes from the schedule. The two numbers not only need not match — they do not even have the same source. Implemented that way in calculate_regimen_case() and held in place by tests.

# day supply comes from the ordered treatment length, not the last dose date
# q48h for 10 days: last dose on day 9, day supply is still 10
day_supply = sum(p.days for p in r.phases)
dose_count = len(admins)
vials = sum(ceil(a.dose_mg / vial_mg)for a in admins)
jcode_units = sum(ceil(a.dose_mg / unit_mg)for a in admins)

Decision D-2 · lock it before refactoring it

The vial rule (ceil per dose, then multiply)was already correct in the code — and it is exactly what a schedule refactor could quietly break. Which is why T-sdv-regression was sequenced before T-phase-model: pin her examples down as tests first, then cut.

Her counter-example is worth memorising verbatim: daptomycin 700mg, 500mg single-dose vials, 30 days → 2 vials/dose × 30 = 60 vials. Not 21,000mg ÷ 500 = 42. That second answer assumes the 300mg left over from one dose can be carried to the next — single-dose vials cannot.

§ 5Layer ④: closing the candidate set

This is the only layer that has never existed, and the only place where the risk of the model fabricating something is real. Q1, Q2 and Q5 all point at the same failure mode:

let the documentation earn the specificity rather than having the AI reason its way into a more specific diagnosis that the chart does not directly support. SME · Q1 · 2026-08-11

A prompt saying "do not over-specify" is not enough. Two structural constraints instead:

@dataclass
class Judgment:
primary: CodeChoice
secondary: list[CodeChoice]
rejected: list[Rejection] # mandatory; an empty list needs a reason too
benefit: "part_d" | "part_b" | "medical" | "unknown"

@dataclass
class CodeChoice:
code: str
why: str
supported_by: Quote # must point back at the chart

@dataclass
class Rejection:
code: str
why_not: str # "deep incisional is not documented"
Decision D-3 · the judgment layer's input must include the benefit

Q5 is explicit: for the same chart, the diagnosis that should lead can differ between a Part D drug authorization and a medical-benefit one. So the benefit is not a by-product of the output — it is an input to the judgment. When it is unknown, this layer should degrade: offer candidates rather than a conclusion.

Decision D-5 · "blank" and "cannot answer" must stay separateOn the submitted form the two arecompletely identical — both are an empty box. But they mean opposite things: one is correct behaviour, the other is.

a risk somebody must see before submission

Corollary: this layer never emits a bare Yes. A Yes either carries its chart citation or it is not a Yes — it is an Unsupported plus a BLOCK-level finding.

Where the questionnaire templates come from

A form is specific to a payer × drug class — Antibiotics CL REQ 05-2026 NV, Humana Electronic PA Form, Caremark Electronic PA Form (2017 NCPDP) — with the version date and even the state baked into its name.

  • Short term — treat the questionnaire as part of the input. The questions are on screen when she submits, so let her (or the PDF intake) bring them in. The system answers questions; it does not have to know which questions exist. That is enough to start without building a template library at all.
  • Long term — a template library plus branch solving. But that depends on A1 (whether a finished PA is pasted into a portal by hand or submitted electronically). If it stays manual, a template library costs more to maintain than it returns; if electronic submission (ePA) is possible, it becomes mandatory.

Decision D-4 · Part B / Part D is a warning, not a decision

Three cases in the library failed here (denied under Part D, eligible under Part B, the payer citing the DME infusion-pump rule). Designed as a flag that cites the rule, handing the choice back to the specialist rather than rerouting automatically. The reason: it fits inside the existing architecture, needs no sixth layer, and does not cross the specialist-in-the-loop line.

§ 8Eval architecture, and a problem that must be solved now

Five dimensions scored per case (extraction / calc / coding / format / safety) already run. Three things to add: bring the 21 cases in; flip the coding dimension from "the extractor must emit no ICD" to "the judgment layer's output vs expected ICD"; and, per §6, add a sixth dimension, cmm — otherwise a third of the output is never scored at all.

How the sixth dimension scores

Comparing answer values alone would grade "correctly blank" and "should have answered but missed it" identically. Compare all three states per question instead: Answered must have the right value and a citation that points at text which really exists; NotApplicable must line up with a blank on the real form; Unsupported must produce its corresponding finding.

⚠ Data leakage · this one is real

The case library is simultaneously the regression suite and the few-shot corpus. If layer ④'s few-shot examples come from the cases it is scored on, the score is meaningless — it is reciting the answers.

The fix: the 21 cases must be split. Suggested 14 few-shot / 7 held-out, committed to the repo and never redrawn.

And say this part out loud: 7 held-out cases means the score has a granularity of 1/7 ≈ 14%, so one case flipping moves it a long way. That number is enough to catch a regression and nowhere near enough to claim an accuracy figure. The second conversation waits for more cases.

§ 9Explicit assumptions: five blanks outside the engine

Every one below is something I have no evidence for and can only assume. They are numbered A1–A5, and that numbering is what the rest of the document refers to. They are written down so that when one is overturned, what collapses is immediately visible.

Two of them change the structure; the other three only change parameters

A1 · once a PA is ready, how does it reach the payer? — pasted into the CoverMyMeds portal by hand, or submitted electronically (ePA)? In the first case the deliverable is a worksheet a person copies from and the system boundary ends there. In the second it needs a submission state machine, receipts, retries and an audit trail — a different system entirely.

A4 · what actually arrives at the start of a case? — how many documents, how many pages, typed or scanned, delivered how? I have never seen a single real referral, so §3's extraction contract and P1's PDF intake are both designed against guesswork.

Neither of these is a detail to be settled later. Both would make the design above structurally wrong. Which is why they lead the follow-up questions to the SME.

§ 11When to come back and change this document

taper A taper.For example q8h for five days, then q12h for five, then q24h for five Three Phase objects. Never average the frequency — that produces the wrong total dose count
SDV / MDV Single-dose vial / multi-dose vial.An SDV is used once and the rest is discarded; an MDV can be kept for the next dose This project uses SDV logic only. So it is ceil(dose ÷ vial size) per dose, then multiplied by the dose count — which is the entire reason 700mg from 500mg vials comes to 60 vials and not 42
wastage
JW modifier		 Wastage.A 335mg dose from a 500mg vial discards 165mg. Some payers require it billed separately with the JW modifier Today this only raises a flag for the specialist. The JW rules were never fully asked about (part of the original Q7)
home infusion Home infusion.The patient infuses at home, themselves or with a visiting nurse — as opposed to inpatient or an outpatient infusion centre It decides two things: whether the benefit is Part B or Part D, and whether per-diem service codes appear at all

B · Three unrelated coding systems

Remember this one line first

ICD says WHY, J-codes say WHAT DRUG WAS GIVEN, S-codes and 99601 say WHO CAME AND GAVE IT.Three systems, three different maintaining bodies, three completely different shapes, answering three different questions. Confusing them is Q6's fifth denial reason.

TermWhat it isWhat it means for the engineering
ICD-10 The diagnosis code — what the patient has. A letter, digits and an optional suffix, e.g. T81.49XA (post-operative surgical site infection, depth unspecified). Used worldwide, maintained by the WHO Layer ④'s output. Never generated — only picked out of a closed set built from quoted chart text
HCPCS / J-code The billing code — what drug, and how many "units". Everything starting with J is an injectable. Each code carries its own unit definition: J0692 = cefepime per 500mg Units = ceil(dose ÷ mg per unit).A separate calculation from the vial count — one vial is not one unit. A 2g dose is 1 vial but 4 J0692 units
S9500 S9501 S9502 Per-diem service codes — a daily bundled rate for the home infusion service (drug, supplies, coordination). Split by frequency: once daily / twice daily / q8h Q4's rule: whether they appear is decided by the benefit type, not by how many drugs there are. A medication-only authorization (CMM / Part D) should carry none
99601 99602 Nursing visit codes — the labour cost of a nurse coming to infuse Removed from AI scope by the SME on 2026-08-03. Now held by one test and two eval bans, and may never reappear
DAW-1 Dispense As Written. The physician requires the brand product; no generic substitution A field on the CMM. This is the question experiment #1 crashed on — the model "inferred" a Yes from context, and the chart contained no such requirement
NDC / GPI Drug identifiers, down to manufacturer and pack size They appear in the case data but are unused today. They matter once a real drug dataset is wired in
